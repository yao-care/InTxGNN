## Meropenem: From Bacterial Infections (Pseudomonas aeruginosa Infection, Meningitis, Peritonitis) to Bacterial Arthritis
{: .text-alpha }

## One-Sentence Summary

Meropenem is a broad-spectrum carbapenem antibiotic, originally used for serious bacterial infections such as *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* infection, bacterial meningitis and peritonitis.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **bacterial arthritis**, but **no clinical trials** are registered for this use.
The **19 publications** retrieved are mostly case reports, retrospective cohorts, susceptibility surveys and laboratory studies, so this is **indirect, low-level evidence**.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Meropenem is a carbapenem, a class that kills bacteria by binding penicillin-binding proteins and blocking cell wall synthesis. Its efficacy in serious Gram-negative and mixed bacterial infections is established, and mechanistically it may be applicable to joint infections caused by susceptible organisms.

Bacterial arthritis is also a bacterial infection, so the new indication is close to meropenem's original uses. The literature supports this plausibility indirectly:
- In a retrospective review of musculoskeletal melioidosis (22 cases), all isolates were susceptible to meropenem.
- Two cases of septic arthritis due to ESBL-producing *Klebsiella pneumoniae* were treated successfully with meropenem plus amikacin and early arthroscopic washout.

This is a plausible mechanistic link rather than demonstrated clinical benefit. No registered trial tests meropenem in bacterial arthritis, and the evidence consists of reports in specific pathogens and settings.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction score is very high (99.92%), but the supporting evidence is indirect: no registered trials, and literature limited to case reports, retrospective series, susceptibility surveys and laboratory studies. Meropenem is also a reserve carbapenem, so stewardship considerations argue against broad use for joint infections without stronger evidence.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Clinical data in bacterial arthritis, such as prospective or comparative studies versus standard therapy, ideally pathogen-specific (for example ESBL-producing Enterobacterales or melioidosis).
- Label-based warnings and contraindications from the India package insert, plus detailed mechanism of action data.
- Joint and bone penetration and dosing data, and a stewardship plan defining the patients for whom a carbapenem is justified.

*Note: another predicted indication for meropenem, complicated urinary tract infection, has considerably stronger evidence (several completed Phase 3 trials, mostly with meropenem as comparator or in combination with vaborbactam). It may merit separate evaluation.*
