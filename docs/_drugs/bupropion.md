# Bupropion: From Depression and Smoking Cessation to Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder

## One-Sentence Summary

Bupropion is an antidepressant originally used for major depressive disorder and nicotine dependence (smoking cessation).
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)**,
with **6 clinical trials** (including a completed Phase 3 placebo-controlled trial) and **20 publications** currently supporting this direction.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Bupropion inhibits the reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine, and it has no serotonergic activity. Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report, but the published reviews retrieved describe this dual catecholamine reuptake inhibition as its main mechanism.

ADHD is linked to dysfunction in noradrenergic and dopaminergic pathways. Established ADHD treatments, such as stimulants and atomoxetine, act on these same pathways. Bupropion's action on them offers a plausible way to improve attention and reduce hyperactivity. It is already used for depression and smoking cessation, and these conditions often occur together with ADHD, so the prediction is clinically coherent.

Reviews describe bupropion as a non-stimulant option for adults who do not respond to, or cannot tolerate, stimulants. Stimulants remain first-line.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications (from knowledge graph associations, not label text)**: seizure disorders (epilepsy), eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa), hypertension, liver failure, cirrhosis and other liver disease, kidney disease, psychotic disorders including schizophreniform and schizoaffective disorders, manic bipolar disorder, substance abuse or dependence including opiate and morphine dependence, myocardial infarction, and central nervous system neoplasms.
- **Drug Interactions**: 665 interactions were recorded. Major ones include opioids (fentanyl, alfentanil, codeine, dihydrocodeine, tramadol), methylphenidate, lidocaine, hydrocortisone, aldesleukin and amantadine. Moderate ones include alprazolam, ethanol, nifedipine, hydrochlorothiazide, cetirizine, chlorpheniramine, acebutolol and alfuzosin.
- **Guardrails identified in the evidence review**: dose-dependent seizure risk, blood pressure monitoring, caution in patients with substance use comorbidity, and the large interaction burden (notably CYP2D6 inhibition).

Package insert warnings and contraindications were not available. Please refer to the package insert for the full safety information.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
A completed Phase 3 placebo-controlled trial, a Phase 2/3 trial, a Phase 4 trial and a Cochrane review support bupropion in ADHD, and the mechanism is plausible. Only one completed Phase 3 trial is confirmed, and bupropion is a second-line option behind stimulants. Seizure risk, cardiovascular effects and heavy interaction burden call for restrictions.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from the India product labels (currently a blocking gap for safety screening)
- Detailed mechanism of action data
- Verification of the comparator and bupropion arm in NCT00048360, and the full outcome data
- A safety monitoring plan covering seizure risk, blood pressure and CYP2D6-related interactions
- Eligibility criteria that exclude patients with contraindicated comorbidities, such as epilepsy and eating disorders

**Other predicted indications:** "ADHD, inattentive type" has only indirect evidence (a post hoc analysis and reviews) and remains a research question. The other eight predictions (for example faciodigitogenital syndrome, chondromyxoid fibroma and hypervitaminosis) have no clinical trials, no meaningful literature and no plausible mechanistic link, so they should be placed on **Hold**.
