## Ketoconazole: Acne (disease)

## One-Sentence Summary

Ketoconazole is an azole antifungal originally used for skin fungal infections (such as tinea, pityriasis versicolor and seborrheic dermatitis) and for some systemic mycoses (such as histoplasmosis and aspergillosis).
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **acne**, but the supporting evidence is thin: **1 clinical trial** (small, no results posted) and a literature set made up mostly of reviews, case reports and laboratory studies.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Ketoconazole is an azole antifungal that inhibits fungal CYP51 (lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase). It also inhibits human steroidogenic enzymes (CYP17A1, CYP11A1), which lowers androgen synthesis. Detailed mechanism-of-action data are not available in the data compiled for this report, so the reasoning below rests on general pharmacology rather than curated drug-database entries.

Two plausible links to acne follow from this:

1. **Less androgen-driven sebum production.** Hyperandrogenism in conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome is associated with acne, and lowering androgens could reduce sebum.
2. **Suppression of Malassezia (Pityrosporum) yeast.** This yeast is implicated in some acne-like or follicular eruptions, including neonatal Malassezia pustulosis and Pityrosporum folliculitis, which is often misdiagnosed as acne vulgaris. Ketoconazole's established use in seborrheic dermatitis, another sebaceous-site disease, supports this link.

Laboratory studies also report that ketoconazole inhibits growth and lipase activity of *Propionibacterium acnes*, the bacterium involved in acne.

The very high TxGNN score is a computational prediction and is not clinical evidence. Because systemic ketoconazole carries liver and adrenal risks, topical use is the only plausible route for acne.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications (from the knowledge graph, not label text):** long QT syndrome (including familial), torsades de pointes, liver disease, gallbladder disease, adrenocortical insufficiency, Addison disease, skin atrophy, telangiectasis and related capillary disease, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, peripheral vascular and arterial disease, intermittent claudication, and achlorhydria.
- **Drug Interactions:** 658 interactions have been identified. Major-level interactions include **loperamide, triamcinolone and budesonide**. Moderate interactions include famotidine, ranitidine, omeprazole, rabeprazole, hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, betamethasone, pioglitazone, aprepitant and atropine.
- **Other considerations:** Systemic use carries risks of liver toxicity and adrenal suppression, so topical use is the only plausible route for acne.

Please refer to the package insert for the full warnings, as label-based safety data were not available for this report.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction is biologically plausible (anti-androgenic and anti-Malassezia effects, plus laboratory activity against *P. acnes*), but the only trial is small, has no results, and is a Phase "not applicable" study of a topical cream. The remaining literature is indirect, so this stays a research question (L4). Label-based safety data are also missing.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Results from NCT07237763, and ideally larger randomized trials of topical ketoconazole in acne
- Confirmation of a suitable topical product and route in India, since most licences are oral tablets
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from the manufacturers
- Curated mechanism-of-action data
- A safety plan limiting use to topical application, given the systemic liver, adrenal, QT and interaction risks
