## Diazepam: From Epilepsy and Anxiety to Insomnia
{: .text-alpha }

## One-Sentence Summary

Diazepam is a benzodiazepine originally used for epilepsy, status epilepticus, alcohol withdrawal, anxiety and tetanus.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **insomnia**, but **no clinical trials** are registered for this use, and the **18 publications** retrieved are mostly reviews, herbal medicine studies and pharmacology papers, with no diazepam-specific controlled trial.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on known information, diazepam is a benzodiazepine that acts as a positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors, and this class has sedative-hypnotic effects. Its efficacy in anxiety and seizure control is established, and mechanistically it may be applicable to insomnia.

Benzodiazepines are already used for short-term insomnia, so the prediction is biologically plausible. However, diazepam has a long half-life and active metabolites (such as nordiazepam). These cause residual daytime sedation, and long-term use carries risks of dependence and other safety concerns. The retrieved literature increasingly points toward deprescribing and newer agents (for example orexin antagonists) rather than expanded benzodiazepine use.

The other predicted indications are weaker still:
- **Sleep disorder, initiating and maintaining sleep** is essentially the same clinical entity as insomnia, and the literature concerns benzodiazepine discontinuation rather than efficacy (L4).
- **Cauda equina syndrome, ADHD (both types), neurogenic bladder and specific developmental disorder** have no direct mechanistic support (L5). For ADHD in children, a report of paradoxical reactions to diazepam is a safety signal.
- **Hallucinogen abuse, antidepressant abuse and barbiturate abuse** have only indirect, symptomatic rationales, and diazepam itself has documented abuse liability (L4).

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindication signals from the knowledge graph** (not label text):
  - Liver failure and liver disease
  - Gallbladder disease
  - Open-angle and angle-closure glaucoma
  - Obstructive sleep apnea and sleep apnea syndrome
  - Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome
  - Respiratory failure and lung disease
  - Myasthenia gravis and neuromuscular junction disease
  - Substance-related disorder and substance abuse/dependence
  - Anxiety, neurotic and dysthymic disorders
- **Drug Interactions**: 295 interactions were found in total. The major one in the listed sample is **morphine** (additive CNS and respiratory depression). Moderate interactions include bupropion, aprepitant, omeprazole, esomeprazole, cimetidine, clarithromycin, cisapride, clotrimazole, miconazole, dronabinol and metoclopramide. Minor interactions include dexamethasone, betamethasone and several antacids.
- **Indication-specific concerns**: long-term benzodiazepine use is linked to dependence, cognitive decline, falls in the elderly and possible breast cancer risk (cohort evidence). Paradoxical reactions have been reported in children with autism.

Please refer to the package insert for warnings and contraindications from the label, which were not available in the compiled data.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The TxGNN score is very high, but there are no registered clinical trials and no diazepam-specific controlled evidence for insomnia. The literature mostly argues against expanding benzodiazepine use because of dependence and residual sedation, and newer hypnotics are displacing the class. Diazepam is already widely marketed in India, so the question is evidence and benefit-risk, not access.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications for the India products, which are currently missing and block safety screening
- Detailed mechanism of action data
- Approved indication text for the India licences
- Controlled trial evidence for diazepam in insomnia versus current standard therapy (CBT-I, orexin antagonists, Z-drugs)
- A dependence and long-term-use risk assessment, including special populations (elderly, sleep apnea, hepatic impairment)
