# Teriflunomide: From Multiple Sclerosis to Relapsing-Remitting Multiple Sclerosis

## One-Sentence Summary

Teriflunomide was originally used to treat multiple sclerosis.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS)**, with **12 clinical trials** and **20 publications** currently supporting this direction.
Note that RRMS is a subtype of the already-approved indication, so this result confirms an existing use rather than identifying a new one.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. From general pharmacological knowledge, teriflunomide is the active metabolite of leflunomide. It inhibits the enzyme dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), which limits de novo pyrimidine synthesis. This reduces the proliferation of activated T and B lymphocytes, the cells that drive inflammatory demyelination in RRMS.

RRMS is the most common form of multiple sclerosis, and it is already covered by the original indication. The mechanism fits the disease biology, and this is consistent with the high TxGNN score. The evidence level reflects the strength of direct clinical support, not novelty.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
Two completed Phase 3 randomized trials (NCT00134563 and NCT00883337) directly support teriflunomide in relapsing MS, which meets L1. Multiple Phase 3 trials of newer agents also use teriflunomide as the active comparator. RRMS is already within the approved indication, so the main task is to confirm the existing use, and the safety data are still incomplete.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from the India product labels, which are currently a blocking gap for safety screening
- Approved indication text for the India licenses, which is missing for all listed registrations
- Detailed mechanism of action data from DrugBank
- A review of the major drug interactions, especially with corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents
